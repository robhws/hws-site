Why certification matters more than test results

A lab test tells you how a filter performed on one day. A certification tells you how it performs for its entire rated life, and then some. That difference is the whole reason I only recommend certified products.

Many "tested" claims come from running a small fraction of a filter's capacity, often at a fraction of its real flow rate, and then presenting that result as if it applied to the whole filter life (Multipure). A fresh carbon filter will reduce almost anything. The real question is what happens near the end of its life, when the media is nearly full.

Certification answers that question. Here is what certification requires that a one-time lab test does not:

What's checkedOne-time lab testNSF/ANSI certification
How much water is testedOften a small sample, sometimes just a few gallonsFrom the first drop through and beyond rated capacity: a 500-gallon filter is tested to at least 1,000 gallons
Flow rateWhatever the lab choosesReal-world flow rates
Who controls the testThe manufacturer can choose what to test and reportThe certifier runs the test; the manufacturer can't influence or stop it
Ongoing oversightNoneAnnual plant inspections and retesting, with recertification every five years
Public proofA PDF from the brandA listing anyone can look up in the NSF, WQA or IAPMO R&T database

Sources: Multipure on NSF certification, Multipure: NSF overview, Multipure: certification and performance.

Testing to double the rated capacity is a built-in safety margin. If you're a month late changing your filter, a certified claim still has headroom. A filter that was only tested new tells you nothing about that month.

Test results still have a place. A lab report on your own tap water tells you what you need to remove. But when it comes to choosing the filter, a certification is proof and a test result is a snapshot.

Reverse osmosis isn't automatically the best

RO is often marketed as the "most pure" option, but purer isn't the same as better. TDS (total dissolved solids) is mostly minerals like calcium and magnesium, and a high TDS reading isn't a health problem on its own.

An RO membrane removes most of those minerals along with the contaminants, and sends part of the water to drain. Certified solid carbon block reduces health contaminants like lead, VOCs and PFAS while leaving the minerals in place and wasting nothing.

RO still has a job. It's the right tool for fluoride, nitrate, very high TDS, and certain forms of arsenic that carbon block isn't certified to reduce. Let your water test decide. If it doesn't show one of those problems, a certified carbon block system is usually the better fit.

Frequently asked questions

Is a certified filter always better than a lab-tested one?

For proving performance, yes. A certification shows the filter met the standard through its rated life, with annual plant inspections and retesting. A lab report shows one result, usually on a new filter. A filter can be excellent and uncertified, but you have no way to verify it.

Does "NSF certified" mean a filter reduces PFAS or lead?

Not necessarily. Certification is claim by claim. A filter can be fully certified for chlorine taste or a few metals and still have no PFAS or lead certification. Check that the specific contaminant appears on the listing.

Does reverse osmosis remove healthy minerals?

Yes. An RO membrane removes most dissolved minerals along with contaminants. Certified solid carbon block reduces health contaminants while leaving minerals in place. RO is worth it when testing shows fluoride, nitrate or very high TDS.

Are WQA and IAPMO certifications as good as NSF?

Yes. NSF, WQA and IAPMO R&T all certify against the same NSF/ANSI standards. The standard and the claim matter, not which of the three issued it.

What's the best filter for well water?

Test first. Most filters here are intended for microbiologically safe water. Well water often needs disinfection or other treatment before a drinking water filter.
