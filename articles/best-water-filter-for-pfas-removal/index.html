How to Test Water for PFAS Before You Buy

Testing is the step most buyers skip, and it's the one that determines whether you need a $350 countertop unit or a whole-house system.

How to use Cyclopure's Purefast™ filter for PFAS Removal

Cyclopure

PFAS testing requires a specialized lab method because these compounds are measured in parts per trillion. Standard home test strips won't detect them. Order a certified lab test through TestMyWaters.com certified lab testing, collect your sample following the instructions, and ship it back. Results typically identify specific compounds like PFOA and PFOS along with other contaminants.

If you're on municipal water, your annual water quality report gives you a starting point, but it reports system-wide averages, not what comes out of your tap. Well water has no reporting requirement at all, so testing is the only way to know.

A common mistake is buying a filter before testing, then discovering the contaminant you're worried about wasn't present in the first place.

EPA PFAS MCL Standards: What the 4.0 ppt Limit Means

The EPA set a maximum contaminant level of 4.0 ppt for both PFOA and PFOS in drinking water. That threshold is the legal limit public water systems must meet, and it's the benchmark to compare against your own test results.

Four parts per trillion is roughly equivalent to four drops of water in an Olympic-sized swimming pool. The EPA also regulates PFHxS, PFNA, and GenX chemicals under related limits. You can review the full rule through the EPA PFAS drinking water regulations.

What matters for buying decisions: a filter that reduces PFAS "below the MCL" isn't the same as one certified to reduce PFAS to non-detect levels. Look for NSF/ANSI 53 or NSF/ANSI 58 certification specifically listing PFOA and PFOS reduction.

Watch Out A filter labeled "reduces PFAS" without an NSF certification number is a marketing claim, not a verified one. Ask for the certification listing before you buy.

Reverse Osmosis vs Activated Carbon for PFAS

Reverse osmosis and activated carbon remove PFAS through fundamentally different mechanisms, and the right choice depends on your test results, your water chemistry, and how much you're willing to spend on maintenance.

Reverse osmosis forces water through a semi-permeable membrane under pressure.

Activated carbon block works by adsorption. PFAS molecules bind to the porous carbon surface as water passes through. Quality carbon block cartridges with tight micron ratings handle PFOA and PFOS well, and they cost less upfront, waste no water, and don't require pressure to operate.

Factor Reverse Osmosis Activated Carbon Block
Removal mechanism Size and charge exclusion at the membrane Adsorption onto carbon surface
PFAS range removed Broad, including many short-chain compounds Primarily long-chain (PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS)
Water waste Yes, reject stream None
Flow rate Slower; depends on pressure and tank Faster; depends on cartridge micron rating
Maintenance cost Membrane plus pre/post filters Cartridge only
Sensitivity to hard water High; scale fouls the membrane Moderate; scale coats adsorption sites
Certification to look for NSF/ANSI 58 with PFOA/PFOS reduction listed NSF/ANSI 53 with PFOA/PFOS reduction listed
Installation Under-sink or countertop Under-sink or countertop

A detail most buying guides skip: the certification standard itself tells you which technology you're looking at. NSF/ANSI 53 covers carbon-based filters making health-effect claims, while NSF/ANSI 58 covers reverse osmosis systems.

Water hardness changes the math on both technologies. Hard water scales RO membranes and coats carbon adsorption sites, shortening cartridge life in either case.

Key Takeaway If your test shows short-chain PFAS or a mix of compounds, RO is the safer bet. If it shows only PFOA and PFOS at low levels and your water is soft, a certified carbon block will do the job for less money and no water waste.

Long-Term Costs and Filter Disposal

The purchase price is the smallest number in the equation. Cartridge replacements, membrane swaps, pre-treatment, and eventual filter disposal add up over a system's life, and most buying guides stop at the sticker price. Here's how to think about the real number.

Build a 5-year total cost of ownership (TCO) before you buy. For any system you're considering, add up:

  • Upfront system cost
  • Replacement cartridges over 5 years (divide cartridge life into 60 months)
  • Membrane replacement if it's an RO system (typically every 2 to 3 years)
  • Pre-treatment filters if your water is hard or has sediment
  • Any professional installation or annual service
  • Disposal or take-back costs at end of life

Run the math on your specific water quality and household usage before assuming the cheaper upfront option wins.

Hard water is the hidden multiplier. Mineral scale coats carbon media and fouls RO membranes, which means cartridges that should last 12 months may need replacing at 6 to 8. A water hardness test belongs in your pre-purchase checklist, and if hardness is high, a softener upstream of the PFAS filter usually pays for itself in extended cartridge life.

Disposal is the part nobody talks about. Spent carbon filters and RO membranes concentrate the PFAS they captured. Throwing a saturated cartridge in the household trash sends that concentrated load to a landfill, where it can leach over time. A few practical options:

  • Check your local household hazardous waste program. Many municipalities accept spent filter cartridges alongside other chemical waste.
  • Ask the manufacturer about take-back or recycling programs. Some brands run them; many don't, and it's worth asking before you buy.
  • If neither is available, double-bag the cartridge in sealed plastic before disposal to reduce leaching risk in the landfill.

This is an area where the industry is still catching up. As of now, there is no universal national take-back program for PFAS-laden filter media, so the burden falls on the homeowner to dispose responsibly.

Pro Tip Before you commit to any system, call your local hazardous waste facility and ask whether they accept spent water filter cartridges. The answer may change which system you buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best water filter for removing microplastics and PFAS?

Reverse osmosis systems are the most effective for removing both microplastics and PFAS. The AquaTru Carafe and Waterdrop G3P800 use RO membranes that capture particles down to 0.0001 microns, which removes PFAS like PFOA and PFOS. For a lower-cost option, solid carbon block filters like the MultiPure Aquaversa are NSF certified for PFAS reduction. Always check for NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 certification to confirm PFAS removal claims.

Will a Brita water filter remove PFAS?

Standard Brita pitchers are not certified for PFAS removal. Brita's basic filters target chlorine, copper, and mercury. Some Brita models with specialized filters may reduce certain PFAS, but you should verify NSF certification specifically for PFOA and PFOS reduction. If PFAS is your main concern, choose a filter with NSF/ANSI 53 certification for PFAS or an RO system, which removes a broader range of contaminants.

Does the EPA have specific standards for PFAS in drinking water?

Yes. In 2024, the EPA set enforceable Maximum Contaminant Levels (MCLs) for several PFAS. The MCL for PFOA and PFOS is 4.0 parts per trillion (ppt) each. The EPA also set limits for PFHxS, PFNA, and GenX at 10 ppt. These standards require public water systems to monitor and reduce PFAS levels below these thresholds. Private well owners should test independently since EPA rules do not cover private wells.

How do I know if my water contains PFAS?

The only way to know is to test your water. Municipal water systems publish annual Consumer Confidence Reports, but these may not list PFAS unless required by state rules. For private wells or detailed PFAS testing, use a certified lab. Healthy Water Solutions LLC offers certified lab testing through TestMyWaters.com. Collect a sample and receive a report showing PFAS levels in parts per trillion so you can compare against EPA MCLs.

What is the cheapest way to remove PFAS from water?

Pitcher filters like the PUR PLUS are certified to reduce PFAS by up to 97%. However, they have limited capacity and require frequent filter changes. For long-term savings, an under-sink RO system like the AquaTru Carafe costs more initially. Compare total cost of ownership over 3-5 years, including replacement filters, to find the best value for your household.

Frequently asked questions

These general answers can help homeowners understand the difference between water sources and treatment strategies.

Is city water better than private well water?

Neither source is automatically better in every situation. Municipal water is treated and monitored by the utility, while private well owners are responsible for testing and maintaining their water supply. The appropriate approach depends on actual water conditions and household priorities.

Should well water be tested before choosing a filter?

Yes. Well water can vary by property, geology, well construction, plumbing and environmental conditions. Testing helps identify which substances or characteristics need to be addressed before treatment equipment is selected.

Does every city water home need the same filter?

No. Municipal water characteristics, household plumbing and homeowner priorities vary. A system selected to improve chlorine taste may be different from one intended to reduce a specific substance identified through testing.

Can a water ionizer replace filtration?

Ionization and contaminant filtration serve different purposes. Source-water concerns should be evaluated separately, and suitable pretreatment or filtration may be required before ionization.

Is whole-home filtration always better?

Not necessarily. Whole-home treatment is useful when a concern affects water throughout the property. Point-of-use treatment may be more appropriate when the primary goal involves drinking and cooking water at one location.
